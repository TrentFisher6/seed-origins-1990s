## Part A. FIPS 186 DSA example (Appendix 5): two exact regenerations

### A1. XSEED (1994) / XKEY (186-1, 186-2) is unseeded BSD `random()` output

**Claim.** The 160-bit value

    bd029bbe 7f51960b cf9edb2b 61f06f0f eb5a38b6

is the first 20 bytes of `random() & 0xff` from a 4.3BSD-family `random()` that was never seeded. That means the default state, which equals `srandom(1)`: TYPE_3 additive generator, degree 31, separation 3, state initialised by `x = 1103515245*x + 12345`, 310 outputs discarded.

**Where the constant is published** (checked against the archived texts, not typed from memory):
- FIPS PUB 186 (19 May 1994), Appendix 5 "Example of the DSA", printed page 18: "x was generated by ... a 160-bit XSEED: bd029bbe 7f51960b cf9edb2b 61f06f0f eb5a38b6".
- FIPS 186-1 (1998), Appendix 5, page 19, and FIPS 186-2 (2000), Appendix 5, page 21: the same bytes, now called XKEY.
- Source texts: `data/agent/raw/fips/fipspub186-legacy.txt`, `fips186-1-csrc.txt`, `fips186-2.txt`, extracted from the nvlpubs/csrc PDFs (the PDFs sit next to them).

**Independent check of the constant.** Recomputing from XSEED gives the x values printed in both documents (`scripts/check_constants.py`, output in `data/check_constants.txt`):
- 1994: x = G_SHA-0(t, XSEED) mod q = `6b2cd935…c8f8ef67`.
- 1998/2000: x = G_SHA-1(t, XKEY) mod q = `2070b322…8b260614`.

**Reproduce.** Run `python3 scripts/reproduce_exact.py` (standard library only, under 0.1 s). It prints the generator's first 20 low bytes and compares them with the constant.

**Why this is not chance.**
- All 160 bits match.
- The search that found it (`c/randomsweep.c`, `data/nrandom_hits.txt`) covered about 2^43 candidates: 2^32 seeds × 64 positions × 4 extractions × 2 initialisations. So the false-match probability is about 2^-117.
- The low byte of this generator depends only on the seed mod 512. The sweep therefore hit every seed ≡ 1 (mod 512), which is expected.
- Seed 1 is the natural reading because it is the unseeded state. 4.3BSD `lib/libc/gen/random.c` (https://www.tuhs.org/cgi-bin/utree.pl?file=4.3BSD/usr/src/lib/libc/gen/random.c) says "Initially, everything is set up as if from: initstate( 1, &randtbl, 128 );". Its static `randtbl` begins 0x9a319039, 0x32d9c024, 0x9b663182, 0x5da1f342, 0xde3b81e0, 0xdf0a6fb5. The `srandom(1)` state computed here, rotated by 28, begins with exactly these words. So a program that never calls `srandom` produces XSEED as its first 20 low bytes.
- glibc's `random()` starts from a different default state, so the machine ran a BSD-derived libc (SunOS 4.x, Ultrix or 4.3BSD).

**Planted control.** A glibc `random()` nibble stream from `srandom(883000000)` with MinghuaQu inserted was found by the same sweep at the right seed and position.

### A2. The FIPS 186-1 SEED is the 1994 SEED + 22

**Claim.**
- FIPS 186 (1994) printed SEED `d5014e4b 60ef2ba8 b6211b40 62ba3224 e0427dbd`, with "counter was at 38". This was under SHA-0.
- FIPS 186-1 and 186-2 print SEED `… e0427dd3`, with "counter was at 105". This is under SHA-1.
- The second is the first SEED ≥ the 1994 SEED, stepping by 1, whose SHA-1 output gives a prime q in the Appendix 2 procedure. With that SEED the procedure finds p at counter 105.
- Seeds +0 to +21 all give composite q. The scan up to +39 finds no other prime q.

**Where the constants are published.** Same appendices and pages as in A1. The SHA-1 replacement example first appeared in FIPS 186 Change Notice 1 (https://public.websites.umich.edu/~x509/ssleay/fip186/186chg-1.htm).

**Reproduce.** Run `python3 scripts/reproduce_exact.py`. It prints the first valid SEED (`…0427dd3`, +22), counter 105 and q `c773218c…ace915f`, which equal the published values. `scripts/check_constants.py` also confirms the printed p and q in 186-1 and 186-2. For 1994 it reproduces counter 38 and the printed q under SHA-0; the 1994 p in the scan is OCR-damaged.

**Interpretation.** When NIST moved the example from SHA-0 to SHA-1, they kept the old SEED and incremented it until the procedure succeeded.

### A3. Still open in the DSA example

- KSEED/KKEY `687a66d9 0648f993 867e121f 4ddf9ddb 01205584` is published and verified: it reproduces the printed k values `79577ddc…` (1994) and `358dad57…` (1998).
- The 1994 SEED `d5014e4b…0427dbd`.
- Neither one occurs in the unseeded `random()` stream. Searched: the first 3,000,000 outputs, byte shifts 0–23 and nibble shifts; 16-, 24- and 32-bit-per-call extractions over 200,000 outputs; TYPE_1/2/4 tables; reversed byte orders over 1,000,000 outputs (`scripts/bsd_reversed.py`). Outputs 20–200 after XSEED fall inside these searches.
- Neither is the 1994 SEED the first valid SEED above a round number: the previous valid SHA-0 SEED is −111.
- For the generators tried on them, see Part C.

## Part B. The "MinghuaQu" seeds (Certicom ECC Challenge 1997, ANSI X9.62 binary examples, SEC 2 r-curves)

### B1. Data and verification
- 89 seeds contain the ASCII string "MinghuaQu" (hex `4d696e676875615175`):
  - 66 Certicom ECC Challenge seeds (seedE/seedP/seedQ, document dated 6 Nov 1997);
  - 10 X9.62 binary example-curve seeds: c2pnb163v1–v3, c2tnb191v1–v3, c2tnb239v1–v3 and c2tnb359v1;
  - 13 SEC 2 seeds: secp112r1/r2, secp128r1/r2, secp160r1/r2, sect113r1/r2, sect131r1/r2, sect193r1/r2 and sect163r1.
- X9.62 c2pnb176w1, c2pnb208w1, c2pnb272w1, c2pnb304w1, c2pnb368w1 and c2tnb431r1 have no seed.
- WTLS curves 4–7 reuse SECG/X9.62 seeds. Side note: the 1999 WTLS spec (p. 84) defines curve 7 as secp160r1 (seed 1053cde4…), but OpenSSL maps wtls7 to secp160r2. Later WTLS revisions were not checked.
- Sources:
  - OpenSSL `crypto/ec/ec_curve.c` (`data/openssl/ec_curve.c`, parsed by `scripts/parse_openssl.py`);
  - SEC 2 v1 PDF text (`data/agent/raw/sec2/sec2-v1.txt`);
  - Certicom challenge PDF appendix and curves list (`data/agent/raw/certicom/`, parsed to `data/agent/certicom.json`).
- `scripts/check_constants.py` confirms that every SECG seed and all 66 Certicom seeds appear verbatim in the archived document texts.
- Every seed checks against its published curve under the X9.62 SHA-1 procedure (`scripts/verify.py`, `data/agent/certicom_verification.json`):
  - prime fields: r·b² = a³;
  - binary fields: b = W, top bit not cleared;
  - challenge points: x = W.
- The exception is sect163r1: plain X9.62 does not give its b. Reversing the 163 bits of W does (`scripts/sect163r1_check.py`). SEC 2 v2 §3.2 (p. 17) already documents this ("the coefficient b produced from S is the reverse of the coefficient … described in ANSI X9.62"), so the seed is genuine.

### B2. How the seeds were built (descriptive; no generating rule found)
1. **Layout.** Each seed is 40 hex characters: a 22-character filler F with the 18-character hex of "MinghuaQu" inserted at character position k, so seed = F[:k] + MQ + F[k:].
   - k takes values 0–21, odd in 45 of 82 distinct seeds. The "4-bit offset" seeds are the odd-k ones.
   - A byte-oriented program would only produce even k, so the seeds were assembled or edited as hex text.
   - Insertion rather than overwriting is supported by sect131r1/r2. They have the same F (last digit 2→3) with MinghuaQu at k=0 and k=11. Overwriting would need an 11-digit coincidence.
2. **Fillers look random.**
   - Uniformity: χ² = 20.7 on 15 degrees of freedom, digit-5–9 fraction 0.312 against 0.3125 expected, about 1,500 digits.
   - No hidden text: the non-MinghuaQu digits contain no more ASCII (either nibble alignment, forward or reversed) and no more decimal-only stretches than random fillers at the same positions (`scripts/meaning_scan.py`; 300-trial null, P = 0.34–0.55).
   - Contrast: some X9.62 binary `a` coefficients and the sect163r1 base-point x are visibly keyboard-typed: c2tnb239v3.a z = 7.2, sect163r1 Gx 6.5, c2tnb359v1.a 5.4, c2tnb191v1.a 4.0 (excess of digits 5–9), and c2tnb191v3.a has runs like `…77e77a777e7e7e77…`. Control (`scripts/typed_control.py`): 59 SHA-1-derived values reach at most z = 2.42.
3. **Fillers are reused and their ends retyped** (`scripts/cluster.py`, `scripts/words.py`, `scripts/chronology.py`, `data/chronology.txt`; 17 components):
   - Identical seeds across documents: the challenge curves ECC2-163/191/238/353 are X9.62 c2pnb163v1, c2tnb191v1, c2tnb239v1 and c2tnb359v1. X9.62 c2pnb163v3 equals the challenge point seed ECC2K-95.seedP.
   - Ends retyped: X9.62 c2pnb163v2 is ECCp-359.seedQ with its first and last digits changed. SEC 2 sect113r2 is c2pnb163v1 with the first byte changed (d2→10) and the last digit changed (4→d).
   - Inside the challenge, families such as ECC2-109/ECC2-353/ECC2K-108/ECC2K-358 (seedP and seedQ) keep the digits next to MinghuaQu. They retype 4–8 leading and 1–8 trailing digits. ECC2-131.seedP begins `b5555555555`.
   - Seeds cross roles: a curve seed in one place is a point seed in another.
   - Reuse links small and large curves, not neighbouring sizes.
   - Null control (`scripts/reuse_null.py`): random fillers at the same MinghuaQu positions give 0.57 edges on average (max 4). Across 200 trials the null edges have 24 or 25 of 40 digits equal (113 edges) or 26 (1 edge), and moved-MinghuaQu edges never occur. The real seeds give 26 edges: 22 with 27–39 equal digits, one each at 24 and 26, and 2 moved-MinghuaQu edges.
   - The only edge into SEC 2 from the older pool is sect113r2. That fixes the direction as 1997/98 pool → 2000 SEC 2.
4. **The same hex-fragment editing appears in other Certicom/SECG parameters.**
   - secp160r1's base-point x (`4a96b5688ef5|7328…`) shares 12 digits with the challenge point ECCp-131 P_x (`03df8|4a96b5688ef5|74fa…`). Chance for any pair in the corpus is about 2e-7.
   - The challenge prime ECCp-359's p ends with 20 digits of ECCp-89's p, with the last byte changed.
5. **First bytes.**
   - The first byte is 0x10 in 5 SECG seeds (secp160r1, sect113r1/r2, sect193r1/r2) and 0x00 in all 4 secp112/128 seeds. Chance of five equal first bytes among 12 random seeds is about 7e-10.
   - Scanning the first or last byte gives no evidence of a counter.
   - Where it can be tested, the published last digits are not "first valid when counting up from 0" (1 of 8 cases for point seeds).
   - Nor are they "+1 from a shared starting value until valid": ECC2-109.seedP's curve already accepts …1942 and …1943, below its …194e.

**Compared with the official account.** SEC 2 (v1 and v2, §2.1 and §3.1) says the r-parameters "were chosen by repeatedly selecting a random seed and counting the number of points … until appropriate parameters were found". The reuse graph shows that in practice a "random seed" often meant an existing MinghuaQu string from the 1997 pool with a few hex digits retyped.

**Best description** of the SECG (and Certicom/X9.62) construction: take a 22-hex-digit filler that looks random but whose origin is unknown. Insert "MinghuaQu" at an arbitrary character position. Make further candidates by retyping a few hex digits at either end, often setting the first byte to 10 or 00 for SEC 2, and reusing fillers across curves and documents, until the curve or point is acceptable. This explains every seed's form and every reuse relation. It does not regenerate the filler digits, so no single rule reproduces the SECG seeds.

### B3. Prior work
- DiSSECT (https://dissect.crocs.fi.muni.cz/standards/secg) notes that the six secp112/128/160 r-seeds share a segment reading "MinghuaQu" and that the choice is unexplained.
- BADA55 (https://bada55.cr.yp.to/vr.html) discusses "verifiably random" seeds in general.
- **Closest prior observation:** a 2013 bitcointalk thread (https://bitcointalk.org/index.php?topic=289795.msg3183975, cited by DiSSECT). There BurtW relays SECG chair Dan Brown: "some of the seeds look similar to each other, and had very non-random looking hex representations". Brown also says that a large middle portion of some seeds contains MinghuaQu, and that "two former employees generated the domain parameters", with archives possibly lost. So SECG had already noticed the similarity qualitatively. New in this report: the quantified reuse graph with a null control, the links back to the 1997 Certicom challenge and X9.62 seeds, the insertion at arbitrary hex-character positions, the retyped ends, and the same editing in base points and challenge primes.
- Apart from that, searches found nothing on the sect* or X9.62 c2* seeds containing the name, on the Certicom challenge seeds, on cross-document reuse, on nibble offsets or on retyped ends.
- For the DSA example, search found only copies of the constants (OpenSSL `fips186a.txt` and Change Notice 1). Nothing explained their origin or linked them to BSD `random()`.

## Part C. What was ruled out (all with planted controls that were recovered)

Targets:
- MinghuaQu fillers, under both the insert and the overwrite model (prefix/suffix runs across the 18-digit gap);
- DSA KSEED and 1994 SEED;
- NIST B-163 seed (secondary).

| Generator / model | Coverage | Script | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| lrand48/drand48, top bits (`>>23`, drand48·2^k), all bit alignments, w = 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 31, 32 | LLL state recovery (PARI qflll) | `scripts/lcg_lll.py`, `run_lll.py`, `lll_sweep.py` | none (P-256 control → 1997-08-16 01:52:34) |
| lrand48 low and middle windows | all 2^(lo+w) low states with lo+w ≤ 34; `srand48(t)` for t from 1989 to 2001, t/2, `>>10..22` | `c/lcgbf.c`, `c/lrand_mid.c` | none |
| `rand()`: ANSI/BSD, MSVC, Borland LCGs; nibble and byte windows | every 32-bit state | `c/lcgbf.c` | 6 hits on 40-bit runs, none extends (noise) |

Statistical note: the hits labelled "noise" are on the shortest runs tested (40 bits). Their counts match the expected false-positive rate, and every one fails on the neighbouring digits (details in NOTES.md).

## Open
- The generator (if any) behind the MinghuaQu fillers. It may have been Certicom's internal RNG or hand-typed hex; the evidence above cannot tell.
- DSA KSEED and the 1994 SEED. Given A1, the remaining candidates are other SunOS-era tools; the libc RNGs are exhausted.
